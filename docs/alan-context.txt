# Alan – culture, organisation, engineering practices and hiring

## Executive summary

Alan is a French-founded, digital-first health partner that combines health insurance and preventive-care services in one experience, operating primarily in France, Spain and Belgium with more than 25,000 client companies, roughly 500,000 members and several hundred employees across Europe.  Its mission is to make it easier for individuals and businesses to actively manage physical and mental health, with a strong emphasis on a delightful, pro-user insurance experience and fast reimbursements.[1][2][3]
Alan’s culture is built around a set of explicit leadership principles – mission-first, member-led, radical transparency, distributed ownership, simplicity and high performance with benevolence – which are meant to be used daily for decisions, communication and hiring rather than as slogans.[4][1]
Engineering is organized as a non‑hierarchical community working in cross‑functional "crews" and larger "areas", using a fairly simple stack (React/React Native frontends, Python/Flask backend, PostgreSQL) and emphasising written communication, ownership, straightforward solutions and extensive automated tests.[5][6][3]
The hiring process is structured, relatively long and heavily aligned with culture: it includes written exercises, live coding or feature design interviews, past‑project discussions and a full "Alan Day" designed to simulate real work while interviewers score candidates against a salary grid and leadership principles.[7][8][9]
For preparing realistic interview exercises, typical patterns include real‑life, data‑heavy problems with business context (claims, reimbursements, pricing), focus on correctness and tests rather than algorithmic tricks, and strong expectations around written reasoning, user‑centric thinking and product awareness.[9][7][5]

## 1. Company overview and mission

Alan positions itself as a health partner rather than only an insurer, aiming to simplify access to care and empower people to be healthier through a combination of insurance, fast reimbursements and preventive digital services.  A recent engineering job description describes Alan’s vision as a world where anyone can actively influence their physical and mental health, with Alan providing prevention, fast reimbursement and support for day‑to‑day health issues in a single experience.[3][1]
The company partners with around 25,000 corporate clients and serves about 500,000 members, with reported annual recurring revenue above 350 million euros and a team of over 580 people across Europe.  It currently operates in at least France, Spain and Belgium, and positions itself as a digital, user‑centric alternative to traditional health insurers.[4][3]

Alan emphasises that its mission – improving healthcare experience and building a "healthy business" – is the primary driver of decisions, a principle summarised as "Mission is the Boss" in its leadership framework.  The company frames insurance as a "pro‑user" service focused on helping members mutualise risk, protect their data and make good health decisions via a beautiful and easy‑to‑use product.[1][4]

## 2. Core values and leadership principles

Alan’s leadership principles are presented as the operationalisation of its culture and are intended to guide day‑to‑day behaviour, interviews and decision‑making rather than remain as high‑level slogans.  The principles include mission‑first focus, member and customer obsession, radical transparency, distributed ownership, disciplined execution and a fight for simplicity, among others.[4]
The company explicitly states that it wants a culture that combines performance, excellence and benevolence, ensuring a strong sense of belonging while keeping high talent density and standards.  Values are expected to be used when prioritising projects, solving member problems and assessing candidates, and are referenced in articles about hiring and candidate expectations.[8][7][4]

Several principles are particularly distinctive:

- **Mission is the Boss** – long‑term mission focus where employees act as owners seeking the company’s success above individual interests.[4]
- **Member & Customer‑led** – obsessive focus on solving customer problems and designing delightful experiences that build trust.[4]
- **Radically Transparent** – information is broadly accessible and written‑first; communication is asynchronous by default while sensitive data remains protected.[10][4]
- **Distributed Ownership & Enlightened Despots** – decision‑makers have ownership and accountability, combining autonomy with responsibility; decisions should be made by those closest to the problem.[5][4]
- **Fight for Simplicity** – preference for simple, pragmatic solutions, elimination of unnecessary processes and concise communication.[5][4]


## 3. Radical transparency and written‑first culture

Alan repeatedly highlights "radical transparency" as a foundation of its way of working, particularly to make flexible, remote‑friendly work sustainable.  The company describes making information accessible by default, sharing decisions in writing, reducing reliance on meetings and publicly sharing both successes and problems in weekly updates.[10][1]
In a French‑language blog about sales culture, Alan explains that salaries are public internally, with a simple grid defined by impact level and total experience; levels are visible to all Alaners to help maintain fairness and avoid long salary negotiations.  The same article stresses a strong culture of written communication where decisions are made in writing rather than in meetings, and every employee has access to essentially all company information, including fundraising discussions, as a sign of trust.[11]

Radical transparency is also tied to performance management and growth: Alan describes a bi‑annual review process where peers, self‑assessment and coaches evaluate behaviours, technical skills and scope of ownership, with hundreds of reviews completed and substantial time invested.  Weekly goals are published by every Alaner, including leadership, and progress towards them is over‑communicated to maintain shared context and accountability.[10]
The leadership‑principles page links radical transparency with written‑first, asynchronous communication and communicates that feedback is candid but delivered with empathy, under principles such as "Empathetic Challengers" and "Kind, Methodic Optimists".[4]

## 4. Organisational structure: areas, crews and communities

On the product side, Alan organises work around two main levels: "Areas" and "Crews".  Areas own a specific part of the product – for example a Claims Management Area for reimbursements, a Health Offering Area for health services, or a Growth & Brand Area for acquisition and marketing – and each area has a "Core team" of four senior Alaners responsible for strategy and success.[6]
Each area comprises two to six "crews", which are cross‑functional teams similar to squads; crews focus on a narrative and concrete commitments for members, clients or internal users, and have a fixed but renewable lifespan.  Crews might focus on topics like fraud prevention, document analysis, online clinic, health‑at‑work programmes or lead optimisation, tailored to their Area’s mission.[6]

In engineering‑focused content, Alan also describes a matrix of "communities" and "crews": every Alaner belongs to one community (for example engineering, design, insurance expertise) and can be involved in one or several crews.  When a new crew is created, a crew lead requests people from different communities to achieve objectives defined using methodologies like OKRs or later, narrative‑based commitments.[6][5]
Crews are autonomous in how they organise themselves; Alan provides a default template but encourages each crew to adapt rituals to its needs, and expects engineers to split time roughly 80/20 between crew work and non‑crew work (such as internal improvements).  The organisation is explicitly described as non‑hierarchical and evolving approximately every two years to support strategy and global expansion.[5][6]

## 5. Product strategy and development process

Alan’s product strategy process combines top‑down direction with bottom‑up input, anchored by a central "Strategy Pack" that outlines yearly vision and strategic priorities.  Areas and crews use the latest version of this Strategy Pack to define their objectives and roadmaps; significant changes are handled via "Product Strategy Reviews" that can adjust Area direction and update the Strategy Pack.[6]
At the Area level, "Product Quarterly Reviews" are conducted at the end of each quarter to reflect on performance, set or adjust 2–5 long‑term KPIs and outline roadmaps for the next six months, with Core teams responsible for these reviews.  Crews then create "Quarter Plans" that translate Area direction into narrative goals, commitments and tasks, including dependencies and risks, aiming for high completion rates during the quarter.[6]

The calendar year is divided into four quarters, each containing two six‑week cycles and starting with a "Cooldown" week, explicitly inspired by Basecamp’s Shape Up methodology.  Mid‑quarter check‑ins allow crews to adjust commitments, while strategy reviews and quarterly reviews update the Strategy Pack, Area priorities and crew Quarter Plans.[6]
Alan emphasises a motto of "We’re here to build products, not overhead", continuously iterating on planning processes to minimise time spent in planning relative to building.  The company also classifies initiatives by maturity – Explore, Expand or Run – and adapts product approaches depending on whether an effort is a new bet, scaling product or mature line.[6]

## 6. Engineering organisation and tech stack

Engineering at Alan is portrayed as central to the mission, with engineers participating in product discovery and definition, rather than only receiving specifications.  Alan engineers are expected to define their own weekly plans in coordination with teammates and to take ownership of development and release of features, aligning with the broader distributed‑ownership principle.[3][5]
The organisation explicitly avoids traditional "Tech Lead" hierarchies; instead, "each of us is a Tech Lead of a part of our stack", and junior engineers can own large scopes while senior engineers are expected to have broader impact.  Engineers are described as "specialists disguised as generalists": every engineer should be able to solve small problems across frontend, backend, data and infrastructure, while remaining free to gravitate towards parts of the stack where they are strongest.[5]

The core tech stack is intentionally straightforward: front‑ends use JavaScript with React/Redux on the web and React Native/Redux on mobile, with some TypeScript used primarily for the design system.  Back‑end services are built in Python with Flask and SQLAlchemy, backed by a PostgreSQL database and Redis for messaging; deployment is done on Heroku and AWS, and the code is organised in two monorepos with two deployed applications, rather than a microservices architecture.[3][5]
Continuous integration and deployment are handled with CircleCI and Heroku pipelines; CI runs many tests for both backend and frontend, and Alan notes that it does not maintain full end‑to‑end tests that span front and back together.  Code reviews are systematic for every pull request, with a bot randomly assigning an additional reviewer to improve knowledge sharing, and technical decisions are made and documented via GitHub issues.[5]

## 7. Ways of working for engineers

Day‑to‑day, engineers participate in written decision‑making processes, creating proposals for technical changes and engaging in asynchronous discussions, consistent with the company‑wide written‑first culture.  Decisions are logged in GitHub issues for traceability, and engineers are encouraged to propose changes themselves rather than wait for top‑down instructions.[10][5]
Alan expects engineers to own their time and outcomes: there is an emphasis on flexibility (work from anywhere, no tracking of working hours) combined with weekly goal‑setting and public progress updates to maintain accountability.  Engineers allocate roughly 80 % of their time to crew‑related work and 20 % to non‑crew work, such as internal tooling, documentation or cross‑cutting initiatives.[3][10][5]

The company supports remote work widely and has adopted a "work from anywhere" policy for many roles, while still supporting in‑person collaboration through offices and co‑working spaces when needed.  The written culture is reinforced by training on decision‑making, clarity of goals and management of notifications to avoid burnout in a highly asynchronous environment.[8][10][5]
Growth is supported by a single software‑engineer career track with levels from C0 up to G and beyond, where promotions are based on demonstrated performance at the target level; levels affect salary and equity but do not limit opportunities to lead crews or act as a coach.[3][5]

## 8. Compensation, equity and transparency

Alan uses a public, non‑negotiable salary grid, where levels correspond to impact and are combined with total years of experience to determine pay; this is described in detail for sales roles but applies company‑wide.  Internal documents emphasise that everyone can see colleagues’ levels, which helps ensure fairness and reduces opaque salary negotiations at the end of hiring processes.[11][8]
Equity is positioned as an important component of compensation: Alan allocates a significant portion of capital to BSPCE (French stock options) for employees, and blog content explains that the value of options for early employees has grown substantially with the company’s valuation.  The leadership explicitly connects this to distributed ownership and long‑term alignment with the mission.[11][10][3][4]

Job descriptions for engineering roles highlight "fair rewards" (equity packages plus salary), flexible working conditions (remote options, flexible hours), generous parental leave and comprehensive health insurance coverage, typically with Alan covering 100 % of contributions for employees and their families in eligible contracts.  This benefits package is framed as an extension of the company’s belief that good health is a basic need starting with employees, not only customers.[3]

## 9. Hiring philosophy and candidate experience

Alan states that the hiring process is designed to be comprehensive and holistic, giving candidates a realistic preview of working at Alan while allowing the company to assess whether they will thrive in its demanding environment.  Official guidance emphasises alignment with culture: if a candidate feels at ease during the process, they are likely to enjoy working there, and vice versa.[7][8]
The company outlines explicit expectations around candidate experience: prompt responses (within 72 hours after applications), quick scheduling (within 24 hours after availability is provided) and feedback within 48 hours after each interview, with the typical process lasting around one month but occasionally compressed to a week.  Diversity and inclusion goals are supported through structured, scripted interviews with clear assessment frameworks, training interviewers on unconscious bias and maintaining the transparent salary grid.[8]

Candidate‑facing content stresses that the hiring process is a two‑way street in which candidates should also evaluate whether they see themselves thriving at Alan, meeting many different Alaners and asking questions throughout.  At the same time, external reviews on platforms such as Glassdoor describe the process as long and demanding, with multiple stages including written exercises, live coding, culture/fit conversations and a full "Alan Day" where candidates spend a half or full day in simulated real work situations.  Some reviews mention eight stages in total and significant time investment (15–16 hours including preparation), but also note detailed feedback and interviews focused on realistic work rather than puzzle‑style questions.[9][8]

## 10. Hiring process for software engineers

The generic Alan hiring guide describes several stages that also apply to engineers: an initial screening interview, one or more technical skills interviews, an "Alaner Fit" interview focused on behaviours and mindset, reference calls, a checkpoint conversation and finally the Alan Day, followed by a decision meeting and offer.  The screening interview aims at mutual discovery, with focus on career history, motivations and understanding of Alan’s mission and culture, and encourages candidates to come prepared with questions.[7]
Technical skills interviews involve case studies and situational questions tailored to the role; guidance encourages candidates to clarify requirements, be honest about gaps, collaborate with interviewers and back their reasoning with data.  The Alaner Fit interview – often conducted by a founder or senior Alaner – assesses mission‑driven mindset, approach to work, communication style, ability to step back and growth mindset in a 45‑minute, scripted conversation.[7]

For software engineers, Alan Day typically consists of three interviews back‑to‑back: a feature‑design interview, a past‑project discussion and an "ask me anything" session with an engineer.  Glassdoor reviews also mention earlier stages specific to engineering: an asynchronous written exercise requiring multi‑page responses, a live coding challenge and additional calls such as a "pre‑Alan‑day check‑in" to align expectations and logistics.[9][7]
During Alan Day, candidates may either work independently on case studies to present later (in some roles) or, for engineers, focus on feature design and in‑depth project conversations; at the end of the day there can be a presentation, followed by a debrief session and social interactions such as lunch with the team.  Interviewers take detailed notes and record which level on the salary grid they believe fits the candidate; a post‑day decision meeting aligns on hire/no‑hire and level, after which offers are made and the level (and associated compensation) is non‑negotiable.[8][7]

## 11. Nature of technical assessments and exercises

External interview reports indicate that Alan’s coding challenges for software engineers are "real‑world" rather than algorithmic puzzles: candidates are asked to implement small classes or functions operating on data, with a strong emphasis on testing and code quality over performance micro‑optimisation.  One candidate describes a real‑case example requiring test‑driven development (TDD) of a solution that an experienced programmer could implement, and emphasises that tests are mandatory.[9]
The live coding stage in some processes is described as implementing a class function, followed by system‑design‑like discussion and multiple culture‑fit interviews, consistent with Alan’s preference for assessing how candidates would work in practice.  Feature‑design interviews during Alan Day expect candidates to think from a user‑centric, product‑driven perspective rather than only backend architecture; one review notes that approaching it purely as a traditional system‑design exercise mis‑matched expectations.[9]
Written exercises often consist of answering several open‑ended questions (for example four questions requiring several pages of written responses) about topics such as motivation, fit with culture, definition of full‑stack engineering, views on testing and areas of expertise.  These asynchronous steps align with Alan’s written‑first culture and help evaluate communication, clarity of thinking and alignment with values.[9]

## 12. Candidate perceptions and critiques

Glassdoor reviews present a mixed picture: some candidates describe the interviews as fair, job‑relevant and rich in feedback, whereas others criticise the length and perceived asymmetry of the process.  Positive comments mention friendly interviewers, clear explanations of expectations at each step, and constructive feedback provided by default after each stage.[9]
Critiques include the number of stages (up to eight plus lunch with the team), the time required to prepare and complete written exercises, and the perception that cultural and mission alignment are repeatedly tested in ways that feel one‑sided.  Some candidates note that questions about motivation for working in health insurance and enthusiasm for Alan’s products are asked multiple times, and that salary level is only revealed at the end despite the emphasis on transparency.[9]
Despite these critiques, reviews commonly agree that technical assessments are realistic and that the process heavily emphasises mission, culture and communication, consistent with Alan’s published principles on radical transparency, member focus and behavioural interviews.[8][4][9]

## 13. Implications for designing realistic practice exercises

Given Alan’s stack and problem space, realistic exercises for software‑engineering preparation tend to involve domain‑rich, data‑processing tasks rather than abstract algorithm puzzles.  Examples include reconciling insurance claims with external payment feeds (such as NOEMIE returns in the French health‑insurance system), computing reimbursements based on complex rules, or generating structured reports for members or internal teams – all tasks that match Areas like Claims Management and focus on correctness, classification and transparency of discrepancies.[12][5][6]
Exercises should mirror Alan’s emphasis on tests and straightforward engineering by requiring candidates to write unit tests (or even encourage TDD), favouring clarity and maintainability over cleverness, and working within languages they use (Python, TypeScript, simple SQL schemas).  The problem statements can explicitly refer to real roles such as crews within the Claims Management or Health Offering Areas, reinforcing cross‑functional collaboration and ownership.[5][9]

Because written communication is central at Alan, high‑fidelity practice also includes tasks where the candidate must explain design decisions in writing, document edge cases, or answer multi‑page prompts about how to approach a feature, how to test it and how it supports member‑first goals.  Feature‑design exercises should force candidates to think from the member’s perspective (e.g. "how does this change the app flow for a member?", "how do we communicate errors or delays?") and not only from the infrastructure or database perspective.[7][10][5][9]
Finally, practice scenarios should integrate culture aspects: prompts that ask candidates to make trade‑offs under "fight for simplicity" and "disciplined execution", to propose written decision documents, or to decide what to share transparently when something goes wrong, match Alan’s leadership principles closely and help candidates internalise how those principles show up in technical work.[10][4]

## 14. Key takeaways for an AI assistant modelling Alan

An AI system trying to emulate "Alan‑style" processes should internalise several patterns. First, it should be mission‑ and member‑first: when generating exercises or feedback, it should always frame decisions in terms of member impact and health outcomes, not only technical elegance.  Second, it should adopt radical transparency and written‑first thinking: explanations of exercises, feedback and decision rationales should be explicit, structured and accessible, with an emphasis on documenting assumptions and alternatives.[1][10][4]
Third, it should mirror the company’s engineering philosophy by preferring simple architectures, clear code, strong test coverage and small, evolvable designs over complex patterns, using stacks similar to React/TypeScript and Python/PostgreSQL when examples are needed.  Fourth, it should structure practice around crew‑like problem narratives, where the candidate is part of a cross‑functional team in a specific Area (for example Claims Management) with clear commitments for a quarter or a six‑week cycle, aligning tasks with Strategy‑Pack‑style goals.[3][5][6]
Finally, the AI should build exercises that resemble the real hiring flow: combinations of written questions, small coding tasks with strong emphasis on tests, feature‑design prompts requiring user‑centric reasoning, and past‑project style reflections, all evaluated through rubrics derived from Alan’s leadership principles and published expectations of candidates.[7][4][9]
